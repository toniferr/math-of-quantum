In 1807 Joseph Fourier claimed, studying the propagation of heat, that any function can be written as a sum of sines and cosines. Two centuries later the discrete version of his idea is everywhere: compressing images, filtering audio, multiplying large numbers. In 1965 James Cooley and John Tukey rediscovered the fast Fourier transform (FFT), which computes the transform of $N$ numbers in $O(N\log N)$ operations. The quantum version is exponentially faster still, with a catch that we will have to learn to get around.

The discrete and the quantum transform

Let $N = 2^n$ and $\omega = e^{2\pi i/N}$, a primitive $N$-th root of unity. The quantum Fourier transform is the linear map defined on the computational basis by

$$\mathrm{QFT}\ket{j} = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \omega^{jk}\ket{k}, \qquad j = 0, \dots, N-1.$$

Applied to a state $\sum_j x_j\ket{j}$, it produces the discrete Fourier transform of the amplitudes, $\sum_k y_k \ket{k}$ with $y_k = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_j \omega^{jk}x_j$.

Proposition (the QFT is unitary)

The columns of the QFT matrix are orthonormal, so it is a valid quantum gate.

Proof

The inner product of columns $j$ and $j'$ is $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\omega^{(j'-j)k}$. If $j = j'$ it is 1. Otherwise $q = \omega^{j'-j} \neq 1$ is an $N$-th root of unity and the geometric sum $\sum_k q^k = \frac{q^N - 1}{q - 1} = 0$.

For $N = 2$, the QFT is exactly the Hadamard gate. And applying $H$ to each of $n$ qubits is the Fourier transform over the group $(\mathbb{Z}_2)^n$: the Deutsch–Jozsa algorithm was already a Fourier transform in disguise.

An exponentially small circuit

The key to its efficiency is that the QFT of a basis state does not create entanglement: it factorizes into $n$ separate qubits. Write $j$ in binary as $j = j_1 j_2 \dots j_n$ and $0.j_l j_{l+1}\dots j_n$ for the binary fraction $\sum_{m\ge l} j_m 2^{l-m-1}$.

Theorem (product representation) $$\mathrm{QFT}\ket{j_1\dots j_n} = \frac{\left(\ket{0} + e^{2\pi i\, 0.j_n}\ket{1}\right)\otimes\left(\ket{0} + e^{2\pi i\, 0.j_{n-1}j_n}\ket{1}\right)\otimes\cdots\otimes\left(\ket{0} + e^{2\pi i\, 0.j_1 j_2\dots j_n}\ket{1}\right)}{2^{n/2}}.$$
Proof sketch

Write $k = \sum_{l=1}^n k_l 2^{n-l}$. Then $\omega^{jk} = \prod_l e^{2\pi i\, j k_l/2^l}$, and the sum over $k$ factors into a product of sums over each bit $k_l \in \{0,1\}$. In $e^{2\pi i j/2^l}$ only the last $l$ bits of $j$ matter, because the others contribute integer multiples of $2\pi i$.

Each factor is prepared with a Hadamard gate and a few controlled phase rotations, $R_k = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{2\pi i/2^k})$. In total $n(n+1)/2$ gates, plus a reversal of the order of the qubits: $O(n^2)$ operations to transform $2^n$ amplitudes, against the FFT's $O(n\,2^n)$. Coppersmith (1994) also showed that the smallest rotations can be dropped without significant loss, leaving $O(n\log n)$ gates.

The catch: the amplitudes are not accessible. The QFT computes the transform, but measuring yields a single value $k$ with probability $|y_k|^2$. It is only useful if the transform concentrates the probability on a few informative values.

Periodicity becomes peaks

That is exactly what happens with periodic states, the situation that matters for Shor's algorithm.

Theorem (transform of a periodic state)

Let $r$ divide $N$ and $m = N/r$. The state that is uniform over an arithmetic progression of step $r$,

$$\ket{\psi} = \frac{1}{\sqrt m}\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}\ket{x_0 + sr},$$

is transformed into a uniform superposition over the multiples of $m$: measuring after the QFT gives $k = \ell\, N/r$ with $\ell$ uniform in $\{0, \dots, r-1\}$, whatever the offset $x_0$.

Proof

The amplitude of $\ket{k}$ is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{Nm}}\,\omega^{x_0 k}\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}\left(\omega^{rk}\right)^s$. Now $\omega^{rk} = e^{2\pi i k/m}$ is an $m$-th root of unity. If $m$ divides $k$ it equals 1 and the sum is $m$, which gives probability $\frac{m^2}{Nm} = \frac{1}{r}$. Otherwise the geometric sum is 0. The offset only contributes the phase $\omega^{x_0k}$, which does not change the probabilities.

Hidden structure in a single query

The same idea gave the first separations between quantum and classical computation, in the model where a function $f$ can only be consulted as a black box.

Bernstein–Vazirani (1993). Let $f(x) = s\cdot x \bmod 2$ for a secret string $s \in \{0,1\}^n$. Classically, each query reveals at most one bit of information about $s$, so $n$ queries are needed. Quantumly, a single one is enough: prepare $H^{\otimes n}\ket{0}$, apply the oracle as a phase $(-1)^{s\cdot x}$, and apply $H^{\otimes n}$ again. Since $H^{\otimes n}\sum_x (-1)^{s\cdot x}\ket{x} = 2^{n/2}\ket{s}$, measuring yields $s$ with certainty.

Simon (1994). If $f(x) = f(y)$ exactly when $y = x$ or $y = x\oplus s$, finding $s$ requires $\Omega(2^{n/2})$ queries for any classical algorithm, even a randomized one, and $O(n)$ for a quantum one: the first exponential separation against randomized algorithms. Daniel Simon's paper is what inspired Peter Shor.

Phase estimation

Alexei Kitaev (1995) reformulated these ideas into a general tool. Suppose a unitary $U$ and one of its eigenvectors $\ket{u}$ with $U\ket{u} = e^{2\pi i\varphi}\ket{u}$. Using $t$ auxiliary qubits, the gates controlled-$U^{2^j}$ write the phase $\varphi$ into the amplitudes as $\frac{1}{2^{t/2}}\sum_y e^{2\pi i\varphi y}\ket{y}$, and an inverse QFT turns it into a binary number.

Theorem (phase estimation)

With $t$ qubits, the measurement returns the closest $t$-bit approximation to $\varphi$ with probability at least $4/\pi^2 \approx 0.405$; with $t + O(\log(1/\varepsilon))$ qubits, an approximation to $t$ bits with probability at least $1 - \varepsilon$. If $\varphi$ has an exact $t$-bit expansion, the result is certain.

Phase estimation is at the heart of Shor's algorithm, of quantum chemistry (estimating energies, the eigenvalues of a Hamiltonian) and of the algorithms for linear systems. The next chapter puts it to work on the problem that made quantum computing famous.
